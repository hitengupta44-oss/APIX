## The headline finding about your archive

**Every row in `Clean_Dataset.csv` was collected on one day: 2022-02-10.**

Recovered by aligning it back to `economy.csv` / `business.csv` (which carry
departure dates) and computing `collection_date = departure_date − days_left`.
Every row returns the same date. Business-class rows align exactly (93,487 =
93,487); economy is off by 108 rows that a sequential walk recovers with zero
misses.

So it is a single-day cross-section across the lead-time curve, not a time
series. It cannot produce the 30-day back-test. It is genuinely good for the
lead-time elasticity module, and it is four years stale — pre-Akasa, with
GoAir and Vistara still flying.

**Start collecting on day one.** Thirty days of your own data is the only route
to the back-test and it costs thirty days of wall-clock time.

## The second finding: your problem statement's premise is out of date

The PS says CPI collects airfares "primarily through manual price-collection
from a limited set of outlets and ticketing offices". That described the CPI
2012 series. It is no longer true.

The CPI 2024 series (base 2024=100), first released 12 February 2026, states in
its own FAQ:

- Q27: airfares are collected through well-known online platforms.
- Q14: airfare, telephone and OTT prices are collected through online platforms.
- The technical note lists air fare among items now using digital and
  administrative sources.

It also uses **Jevons for elementary indices** (Q20) and **Young / modified
Laspeyres above that** (Q21) — exactly the methodology in `apix/index.py`. Cite
this. "Our index uses the same formula family NSO uses" is a strong line and it
is true.

**Reframe the pitch.** Not "replace manual collection" — that argument is
already lost. Instead: NSO collects airfares monthly, as one item inside group
07.3, producing a single national number. APIx adds three dimensions the CPI
item index structurally cannot carry:

- **Frequency** — daily versus monthly, so RBI sees a fare shock in days.
- **Route granularity** — 30 directional city pairs versus one national figure.
- **Lead-time structure** — the T+1 to T+45 curve, where the 200-400%
  dispersion actually lives and which no single monthly price can express.

That is a supplement to an existing official series rather than a replacement
for a broken one, which is a far easier thing for MoSPI to adopt.

## RESOLVED

### 1. DGCA domestic city-pair passenger traffic → route weights

The blocking gap, and it was one file away from data you already had. Your
`city.csv`, `carrier.csv` and `daily.csv` all come from
`github.com/Vonter/india-aviation-traffic`; you pulled the **international**
city file. The domestic one sits in the same repo.

- File: `data/dgca_domestic_city.csv` (3.8 MB, 65,166 rows)
- Coverage: 2015-01 to **2026-05**, 2,145 city pairs, monthly
- Licence: ODbL, attribute DGCA
- Generator: `python -m scripts.build_weights --refresh --months 12 --top 30`

Output is `config/route_weights.yaml`, wired into `config/basket.yaml` as
version `dgca-2026-05-top30`. Trailing 12 months (2025-06 to 2026-05), city-name
mapping covers **94.8%** of domestic passengers, and the top-30 basket covers
**26.8%** of all domestic traffic.

Top weights: BOM-DEL 7.51%, DEL-BOM 7.47%, BLR-DEL 5.44%, DEL-BLR 5.40%,
BOM-BLR 4.63%.

Worth noting how far off the placeholders were: I had guessed DEL-BOM at 11.8%
against an actual 7.47%, a 58% error on the largest weight in the basket. That
is why the placeholder version was not publishable.

Two decisions to be ready to defend:

- DGCA reports each *unordered* pair once, with traffic in both directions.
  APIx prices *directional* routes because DEL-BOM and BOM-DEL are separately
  priced products, so each DGCA row becomes two weighted routes.
- Weights are a 12-month trailing sum, not one month. A single month bakes that
  month's seasonality into a weight that then multiplies every future
  observation, double-counting it. Twelve months is also what NSO uses as a
  weight reference period.

Source quirks the script handles, and which will silently drop routes if you
write your own: Goa appears as both `GOA (DABOLIM, SOUTH GOA)` and `GOA (MOPA,
NORTH GOA)` since Mopa opened; Mumbai now splits into `MUMBAI (MUMBAI)` and
`MUMBAI (NAVI MUMBAI)`; DGCA renames entries between releases.
`normalise_city()` strips the parenthetical and falls back to the parent city
rather than losing the row. Fixing this alone moved coverage from 82.1% to
94.8%.

**Verify a few months against the DGCA PDFs before publishing.** This is a
community parse of the primary source — good, but not the primary source.
Primary: DGCA monthly domestic traffic reports, Table 3, at
`dgca.gov.in/digigov-portal/?page=monthlyStatistics/259/4751/html`.

### 2. CPI weights and classification

From the CPI 2024 first release (12 Feb 2026), division-wise weights:

| Division | Rural | Urban | Combined |
|---|---|---|---|
| 07 Transport | 8.644 | 8.985 | **8.796** |
| 08 Information and communication | 3.647 | 3.563 | 3.609 |

Under CPI 2012 the Transport share was 6.394 combined, so transport's weight
rose by roughly a third. Worth a slide: airfare movements now matter more to
headline CPI than they did under the old series.

Airfare sits in group **07.3 Passenger transport services**. February 2026
index for 07.3 combined was 103.57 (+1.75% y/y) against Transport overall at
100.69 (−0.05%) — passenger transport was already running well above its own
division.

Item-level weights, including the air fare item, are published at
`cpi.mospi.gov.in` under the announcement tab. Get that number. It is what lets
you say "a 1% APIx move implies X basis points on headline CPI", which is the
single most quotable output this project can produce.

Monthly index data: `esankhyiki.mospi.gov.in/macroindicators?product=cpi`

One constraint: the 2012 and 2024 series can be linked **only at general index
level** (linking factors: rural 0.5222, urban 0.5320, combined 0.5267). There
is no way to build a long transport back-series across the break. Do not try.

### 3. Event and seasonality calendar

- Generator: `python -m scripts.build_calendar --from 2026-09-01 --to 2027-03-31`
- Output: `data/event_calendar.csv`

Festival dates in India are lunar and move 10-20 days a year, so they are
computed with the `holidays` package rather than hardcoded. Hardcoding is how
these calendars silently rot.

Events carry asymmetric travel windows (Diwali: 7 days before, 6 after) because
demand clusters around an event rather than on it, and outbound and return legs
are not symmetric. Flags attach to the **departure** date, not the collection
date — a fare collected on 25 Oct for an 8 Nov departure is a Diwali fare.

One caveat the script now warns about: with the broad seasonal windows
included, `is_surge` fires on ~73% of days, giving it no discriminating power.
Use the individual `season_*` and `event_window` columns instead. "Fares rose
because of Diwali" and "fares rose because it was a long weekend" are different
claims and you will be asked which one it was.

## STILL OPEN

### 4. Booking lead-time distribution — the weakest link

The `apw_weights` in `basket.yaml` are still invented. India publishes no
booking curve. In order of preference:

1. Ask an OTA for an aggregate booking-curve disclosure. They have it, and a
   government-badged student project asking for aggregate percentages is not an
   unreasonable request.
2. Cite an IATA or OAG lead-time study, adjusted for the Indian domestic mix.
3. **Publish a sensitivity table** across three profiles — front-loaded, flat,
   back-loaded — showing how much APIx moves under each.

Do option 3 regardless of whether 1 or 2 lands. Turning an unavoidable
assumption into a documented sensitivity is stronger than a confident wrong
number, and it is what a statistical agency would actually do.

### 5. DGCA monthly average-fare series — the back-test benchmark

Named explicitly in the problem statement, and not available in
machine-readable form. DGCA publishes fare monitoring inside its monthly
reports; there is no clean CSV. Budget time to hand-enter 12 months from the
PDFs, and do it early. Discovering in week three that your benchmark is not a
download is a bad week.

### 6. UDF and ASF schedules with effective dates

`normalize.py` hardcodes UDF per airport and ASF at ₹236. Real values come from
AERA tariff orders and AAI circulars, and they change. **The effective date
matters as much as the value**: when UDF changes at BLR, every fare
decomposition before that date used a different constant, and without dated
values your ex-tax back-series breaks silently at the change point.

### 7. ATF prices

- `data.gov.in` — IOCL ATF for domestic airlines, Delhi, 2022-04 to 2025-03
  (dated series, good for back-fill)
- `iocl.com/atf-domestic-airlines-on-international` — current metro prices
- PPAC (`ppac.gov.in`) — the source MoSPI itself uses for fuel prices in CPI,
  which makes it the defensible citation

Prices revise on the 1st of each month, metro-wise. As of 1 March 2026: Delhi
₹96,638/KL (+5.7% m/m), Mumbai ₹90,452, Kolkata ₹99,587, Chennai ₹1,00,280.
The best single explanatory variable available for fare movements, and it turns
the dashboard from descriptive into analytical.
